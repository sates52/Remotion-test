```
She doesn't want to save him. She wants first-person access — and she'd tell you that herself, out loud, without flinching.

That's Fruit Fly by Josh Silver: Mallory Maddox, seven years into writer's block, finds Leo — a young addict sleeping rough and trading sex to survive — and decides his worst year is her next bestseller. This is the deep read, and the argument is simple and brutal: her block was never a creative problem, it was a SUPPLY problem. She never wrote from imagination. She wrote from access.

The phrase that runs through the whole breakdown: self-awareness is not a brake, it's an alibi. Mallory names her own pattern with clinical precision — and the naming becomes her permission slip. We trace how the first-person voice makes you complicit (you laugh with her, so you agree with her), why the title indicts her twice (the woman orbiting a gay man's life, and the insect that hovers where fruit is already going bad), how two addictions turn out to share one mechanism, and the point where the satire becomes a thriller: a narrator who needs a third act starts managing reality to get one.

Then we turn the lens around. A satire about selling someone's pain, itself sold as a novel and optioned for prestige TV — is the complicity the point, or the escape hatch? Because we just spent 35 minutes consuming a story built out of someone's worst year. And we enjoyed it.
```
