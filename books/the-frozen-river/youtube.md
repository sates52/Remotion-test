## Description

```
A body is frozen into the Kennebec River. The town won't let a woman testify in court — but they need her to read the corpse. That's the paradox at the heart of The Frozen River by Ariel Lawhon.

In this deep dive we make the case that the murder mystery is a decoy. The real subject is evidence: in 1789 Maine, who is allowed to produce it, and who is allowed to be believed? Midwife Martha Ballard — whose diary is a real historical document — can read a dead body and keep the only reliable record in town, yet her word means nothing against two of the most powerful men in the county. We trace how she wins not with a weapon or a speech, but by writing everything down.

We cover the forensic examination of Colonel Joshua Burgess, Rebecca Foster's rape accusation against Judge Joseph North, the doctrine of coverture that erased a married woman's testimony, the clash between Martha's hands-on knowledge and Dr. Page's credentialed guessing, the trial and what the court chose to ignore — and one honest criticism of how modern the novel lets Martha be.
```
