The most quoted line in Paradise Lost is a lie — and its speaker is the first person it destroys.

Satan says "The mind is its own place, and can make a Heav'n of Hell, a Hell of Heav'n" while standing on a burning lake, minutes after the worst defeat in the universe. He hasn't even stood up yet. Milton then spends ten thousand lines proving he cannot live inside that sentence — and hands the same idea, corrected, to two ruined humans at the very end.

So this isn't a war story between angels and demons. It's a manual on self-persuasion: how a mind builds an airtight case for what it already wants, and keeps running that case long after reality has disproved it.

We get into the blind, politically defeated poet dictating an infinite cosmos in the dark; the 18th-century scholar who invented a "phantom editor" rather than admit Milton was smarter than him; the rigged council in Pandemonium where the vote was theater; the private confession on Mount Niphates where the performance stops and the slogan collapses into "Myself am Hell"; Abdiel standing alone as Milton's self-portrait; Eve winning the separation argument using Milton's OWN free-speech logic from Areopagitica; the serpent's three-step rhetoric clinic; and Adam, who is not deceived and eats anyway.

Plus the honest criticism: by his own scoreboard — "justify the ways of God to men" — many readers think Milton loses. God argues like a defense attorney. Satan talks like a person. Blake said Milton was of the Devil's party without knowing it.

One phrase runs through the whole thing: the mind is its own place.
