The person who got the top score probably didn't travel the farthest to get it — and we just handed the prize to the wrong thing.

That is the uncomfortable idea at the heart of "Hidden Potential: The Science of Achieving Greater Things" by Adam Grant. We grade people at the wrong moment, with the wrong ruler — rewarding the height of the peak and ignoring the distance someone climbed to reach it. And in doing so, we waste most of the talent around us.

In this deep dive we unpack why character skills — not raw IQ — are the real engine of growth, how the best learners become "creatures of discomfort," why being a sponge beats being a filter, and how scaffolding from coaches, schools, and teams decides whose potential is ever seen. Real cases along the way: Harlem's Raging Rooks chess team, Maya Shankar's pivot from violin prodigy to scientist, R.A. Dickey reinventing himself with the knuckleball, and the kindergarten study that predicted adult success.

We also sit honestly with the counterpoint — how "you have hidden potential" can get weaponized into pressure — before the payoff: stop asking how talented someone is, and start asking how far they could travel, and what scaffolding we're failing to build.

Measure the distance traveled, not the height of the peak. No fluff, no ending spoilers.
