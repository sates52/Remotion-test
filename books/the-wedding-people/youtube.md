## Description

```
She books the nicest room of her life to end it — and the first person she tells is the bride.

That's the collision at the heart of The Wedding People by Alison Espach. A six-day luxury wedding is a performance machine — everyone auditioning "the happy version" of themselves — and Phoebe is the one guest who has stopped performing, because she's decided to die. This is the deep read: despair here isn't the absence of a good life, it's the exhaustion of performing one, and the cure isn't happiness — it's honesty with strangers who have no stake in your story.

We trace the whole argument: the confession in the elevator that cracks the plan, why the controlling bride and the suicidal guest are secretly the same person, the stranger paradox (why you tell the truth to people you met yesterday), the un-lived life of a woman who became a reader of her own story instead of its writer, and the quiet permission to stay. We also sit honestly with the hardest critique — is "honesty plus a gorgeous vacation" really a cure for clinical despair, or a comforting fantasy? The payoff is smaller and truer than "life is beautiful": you don't have to want your whole life back, you just have to want tomorrow.
```
